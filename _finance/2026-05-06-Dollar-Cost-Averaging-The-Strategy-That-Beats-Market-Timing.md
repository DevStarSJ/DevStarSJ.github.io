Everybody wants to buy low and sell high. In theory, it's the whole game. In practice, no one — not hedge funds, not financial advisors, not Nobel Prize winners — can consistently predict market tops and bottoms. The solution isn't a better crystal ball. It's a strategy that makes market timing irrelevant: **Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)**.

## What Is Dollar-Cost Averaging?

Dollar-cost averaging means investing a **fixed dollar amount at regular intervals**, regardless of what the market is doing.

Instead of trying to invest $12,000 at the "perfect" moment, you invest $1,000 every month for 12 months. When prices are high, you buy fewer shares. When prices are low, you buy more. Over time, this averages out your cost per share.

**Example:**

| Month | Investment | Share Price | Shares Purchased |
|-------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Jan | $1,000 | $50 | 20.0 |
| Feb | $1,000 | $40 | 25.0 |
| Mar | $1,000 | $30 | 33.3 |
| Apr | $1,000 | $35 | 28.6 |
| May | $1,000 | $45 | 22.2 |
| Jun | $1,000 | $50 | 20.0 |
| **Total** | **$6,000** | **Avg: $41.67** | **149.1 shares** |

Average price paid per share: **$40.24** (vs. average market price of $41.67)

You naturally bought more shares when prices were low — without trying to time anything.

## Why It Works

### 1. It Removes Emotion
The #1 enemy of investors is themselves. Fear causes people to sell at the bottom. Greed causes them to pile in at the top. DCA automates the decision so you don't have to battle your own psychology every month.

### 2. It Solves the Timing Problem
Studies consistently show that even professional fund managers fail to beat the market through timing. One famous study found that missing just the **10 best days** in a 20-year period cut returns in half.

With DCA, you're always invested. You'll catch those best days automatically.

### 3. It Makes Crashes Work For You
Market downturns feel terrifying. But when you're DCA-ing, a 30% crash means your monthly $1,000 buys 43% more shares than before the crash. Volatility becomes your friend, not your enemy.

### 4. It Builds the Habit
Automating investments means it happens whether you feel like it or not. This consistency, over decades, is what actually builds wealth.

## DCA vs. Lump Sum Investing

Here's the honest comparison: statistically, **lump-sum investing beats DCA about 67% of the time**, because markets trend upward over time — money invested sooner has more time to grow.

However:
- Most people don't have large lump sums sitting in cash
- DCA dramatically reduces the risk of investing at the worst possible moment
- The emotional comfort of DCA leads to better long-term behavior
- For regular income earners (ie., everyone with a paycheck), DCA is the natural strategy

**Bottom line:** If you have a lump sum and a long time horizon, investing it all at once is statistically better. If you're investing from regular income, DCA is the right approach.

## How to Set Up DCA

### Step 1: Choose Your Investment
DCA works best with broad index funds or ETFs:
- **US Total Market:** VTI (Vanguard), ITOT (iShares)
- **S&P 500:** VOO, SPY, IVV
- **International:** VXUS, IXUS
- **Target Date Funds:** One-fund solution that auto-rebalances

### Step 2: Determine Your Amount
Start with what you can sustain. $100/month consistently beats $1,000/month that stops after 3 months. Aim for 15–20% of take-home pay.

### Step 3: Set a Schedule
Monthly is the standard. Bi-weekly syncs with paychecks. Weekly for smaller, more frequent investors. Frequency matters less than consistency.

### Step 4: Automate
Set up automatic contributions so you never have to decide:
- **401(k)/403(b):** Automatically taken from paycheck
- **IRA:** Set up automatic transfers to Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab
- **Taxable account:** Automate monthly purchases

### Step 5: Don't Touch It
The final — and hardest — step. When markets crash, your DCA plan keeps buying. When markets surge, it keeps buying. Let time and compounding do the work.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

❌ **Pausing contributions during downturns** — This is when DCA is most powerful  
❌ **Increasing contributions only in bull markets** — You're then lump-summing at highs  
❌ **Stopping to "wait for a better price"** — This is timing in disguise  
❌ **Using DCA with individual stocks** — High volatility in single stocks is risky; use diversified funds  

## The Long Game

A 25-year-old investing $500/month in an S&P 500 index fund with a historical 10% average annual return would have approximately:
- Age 35: ~$102,000
- Age 45: ~$379,000
- Age 55: ~$1,135,000
- Age 65: ~$3,163,000

No timing. No complexity. Just consistency.

## Bottom Line

Dollar-cost averaging isn't glamorous. There's no newsletter to subscribe to, no hot tips to follow, no algorithms to decode. That's precisely why it works. It turns investing into a habit rather than a decision — and good habits, compounded over decades, are what actually build wealth.

Set it up. Automate it. Ignore the noise.
