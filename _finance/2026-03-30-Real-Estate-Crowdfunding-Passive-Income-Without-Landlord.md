Owning rental property sounds great until the 2 AM call about a broken furnace, a tenant who hasn't paid rent in three months, and a surprise $8,000 roof replacement.

**Real estate crowdfunding** gives you the income benefits of real estate investing — without the landlord headaches.

Here's how it works, which platforms are worth using, and realistic expectations for what you can earn.

---

## What Is Real Estate Crowdfunding?

Real estate crowdfunding pools money from many investors to fund real estate projects — apartment buildings, commercial properties, single-family rentals, industrial facilities, even hotels.

Instead of buying a property yourself, you invest a smaller amount alongside hundreds or thousands of other investors. A professional management team handles everything: acquisition, renovation, tenants, maintenance, and eventual sale.

You receive a share of:
- **Rental income** (distributed quarterly or monthly)
- **Appreciation** (when the property eventually sells)

It's real estate ownership — just fractional, passive, and professionally managed.

## Real Estate Crowdfunding vs. REITs

You may already know about REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts) — publicly traded companies that own real estate portfolios. Both REITs and crowdfunding give real estate exposure without direct ownership. Here's how they compare:

| | REITs | Real Estate Crowdfunding |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | Trade daily on stock market | Illiquid, locked up 1–7 years |
| **Minimum** | Price of 1 share (~$10–50) | $10–$25,000+ depending on platform |
| **Returns** | Correlates with stock market | More stable, less correlated |
| **Control** | None | Slightly more (choose specific deals) |
| **Accreditation** | Not required | Some platforms require it |
| **Transparency** | SEC filings, quarterly reports | Varies by platform |

**When to use REITs:** You want maximum liquidity and low minimums.  
**When to use crowdfunding:** You want less stock market correlation and can lock up capital.

## Top Real Estate Crowdfunding Platforms

### Fundrise — Best for Beginners
**Minimum:** $10  
**Who can invest:** Anyone (non-accredited)  
**Historical returns:** ~7–12% annualized (varies by period)  
**Liquidity:** Quarterly redemption windows (not guaranteed)

Fundrise is the most accessible option. You invest in "eREITs" — diversified portfolios of residential and commercial properties. Their app is excellent, fees are low (0.85% annual advisory + 0.15% management), and they've delivered solid returns over more than a decade.

Best for: First-time real estate investors who want simplicity.

---

### Arrived — Best for Single-Family Rentals
**Minimum:** $100  
**Who can invest:** Anyone  
**Returns:** Rental income + appreciation (historically 8–15% total)  
**Liquidity:** 5–7 year hold, secondary market available

Arrived (formerly Arrived Homes, backed by Jeff Bezos) lets you buy fractional shares of individual rental homes. You see the specific house, location, and projected returns before investing. Rental income paid quarterly.

Best for: Investors who want to pick specific properties they understand.

---

### RealtyMogul — Best for Accredited Investors
**Minimum:** $5,000  
**Who can invest:** Accredited investors (non-accredited for REITs)  
**Returns:** 6–12% depending on deal type  
**Liquidity:** 3–5 year hold typical

RealtyMogul focuses on commercial real estate — multifamily apartments, office, retail. Deals are pre-vetted. Their two non-accredited REITs (MogulREIT I and II) start at $5,000.

Best for: Investors ready to put more capital into specific commercial deals.

---

### Crowdstreet — Best for Large Commercial Deals
**Minimum:** $25,000  
**Who can invest:** Accredited investors only  
**Returns:** Target 15–25% IRR on individual deals  
**Liquidity:** 3–10 year hold

Crowdstreet offers direct access to individual commercial real estate deals — major apartment complexes, office buildings, hotels. Higher risk and higher potential return than diversified platforms.

Best for: Sophisticated investors with larger portfolios seeking higher returns.

---

### Groundfloor — Best for Short-Term Lending
**Minimum:** $10  
**Who can invest:** Anyone  
**Returns:** 7–12% interest  
**Liquidity:** 6–12 month loans

Groundfloor is different: instead of equity, you're lending money to real estate developers (fix-and-flip projects). You earn fixed interest, and the loan pays off when they sell the property. Shorter timeline, no equity appreciation, but more predictable income.

Best for: Investors who want faster liquidity and fixed-income-style returns.

## Tax Treatment of Crowdfunding Returns

Real estate crowdfunding income has favorable tax treatment compared to ordinary income:

**Rental income distributions:** Taxed as ordinary income, but often offset by **depreciation** passed through to investors. In many cases, investors receive paper "losses" from depreciation that offset the income entirely.

**Capital gains:** When a property sells, profits qualify for long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income level) if held over a year.

**1099 forms:** Most platforms issue 1099-DIV or 1099-INT. Some deals may issue K-1s (partnerships), which are more complex for taxes.

**Invest inside an IRA:** Some platforms (Fundrise, others) allow investment in Self-Directed IRAs, letting returns compound tax-deferred or tax-free.

## The Risks to Understand

Real estate crowdfunding is not risk-free:

**Illiquidity:** You may not be able to exit early. If you need cash, you might be stuck waiting for the property to sell. Never invest money you might need.

**Platform risk:** If the platform goes out of business, your underlying real estate assets should still be intact — but access and management become complicated.

**Deal risk:** Individual properties can underperform. Tenants leave, markets shift, development projects run over budget.

**Interest rate risk:** Rising interest rates make real estate less attractive and can reduce property values.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Diversify across multiple platforms and deal types
- Start with diversified funds (Fundrise) before individual deals
- Only invest money you don't need for 3–7 years
- Start small and learn before increasing exposure
