## What Is an Index Fund?

An index fund is a type of investment fund that tracks a specific market index — like the S&P 500, which represents the 500 largest U.S. companies. Instead of a fund manager actively picking stocks, an index fund simply *buys all the stocks in the index* in the same proportions.

**Examples of popular indexes:**
- **S&P 500** — 500 largest U.S. companies (Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, etc.)
- **Total Stock Market** — essentially every U.S. publicly traded company (~4,000 stocks)
- **Total International** — stocks from markets outside the U.S.
- **Total Bond Market** — U.S. bonds of various types

## Why Index Funds Beat Active Management

This is where the data gets compelling. The S&P Indices Versus Active (SPIVA) report tracks how actively managed funds perform versus their benchmark indexes. The findings are consistent and striking:

> **Over 15 years, approximately 90% of actively managed large-cap U.S. funds underperform the S&P 500.**

Why? Three reasons:
1. **Costs:** Actively managed funds charge 0.5–1.5% expense ratios. Index funds charge 0.03–0.20%.
2. **Human error:** No fund manager consistently predicts the market correctly
3. **Tax drag:** Active trading creates taxable events; index funds rarely sell

### The Math of Fees Over Time

| Starting Amount | Annual Return | Expense Ratio | Value After 30 Years |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10,000 | 8% | 1.0% (active) | $66,100 |
| $10,000 | 8% | 0.03% (index) | $99,300 |

The 0.97% difference in fees costs **$33,200** over 30 years on a $10,000 investment. On a $100,000 portfolio, that's $332,000 lost to fees.

## The Three-Fund Portfolio: The Simplest Smart Strategy

Legendary Bogleheads (followers of Vanguard founder Jack Bogle) recommend a simple three-fund portfolio:

1. **U.S. Stocks:** VTI or FSKAX (~60%)
2. **International Stocks:** VXUS or FTIHX (~30%)
3. **Bonds:** BND or FXNAX (~10%)

Adjust the stock/bond ratio based on your age and risk tolerance. Younger investors can hold more stocks; those near retirement shift more toward bonds.

## Dollar-Cost Averaging: The Beginner's Secret Weapon

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, regardless of market conditions.

**Why it's powerful for beginners:**
- You buy more shares when prices are low, fewer when high
- Removes the emotion of trying to "buy the dip"
- Automates the process — set it and forget it
- Works perfectly with payroll deduction (401k) or automatic transfers

**Example:** Invest $500/month into VOO every month, regardless of market conditions. Over 20 years at historical rates, this grows to approximately **$294,000**.

## Common Index Fund Myths Debunked

**"Index funds are boring/mediocre"**
→ Returns in the top 10% of all investments over any long period. "Boring" beats "exciting" in investing.

**"I can pick better stocks"**
→ Studies show most individual investors (and professional fund managers) underperform index funds over 15+ years.

**"Index funds have too much concentration in big tech"**
→ The S&P 500 is market-cap weighted, so large companies have higher weight. But it still holds 500 companies across all sectors.

**"Now isn't a good time to invest"**
→ Research consistently shows that investing now (even at a "peak") outperforms waiting for the "right time."

## Getting Started in 3 Steps

1. **Open an account:** Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab all offer excellent index funds with zero account minimums
2. **Choose your fund:** Start with a single fund like VOO, VTI, or a target-date fund if you want simplicity
3. **Set up automatic investing:** Monthly auto-invest of any amount. Even $50/month compounds significantly over decades.
