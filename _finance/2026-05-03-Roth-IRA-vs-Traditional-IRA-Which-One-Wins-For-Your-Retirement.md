Choosing between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA is one of the most important retirement decisions you'll make — and the wrong choice could cost you tens of thousands of dollars over time. Here's how to figure out which account wins for *your* situation.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay Taxes

Both accounts offer tax advantages — they just apply at different times.

| | Roth IRA | Traditional IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Contributions | After-tax dollars | Pre-tax (deductible) |
| Growth | Tax-free | Tax-deferred |
| Withdrawals | Tax-free | Taxed as income |
| RMDs | None | Required at age 73 |

## Who Should Choose the Roth IRA?

**You're young or early in your career.** If you're in the 22% tax bracket now but expect to be in the 32% bracket at retirement, paying taxes today is a bargain.

**You want tax-free income in retirement.** Roth withdrawals don't count as income, which means they won't push your Social Security into taxable territory.

**You want flexibility.** You can withdraw your *contributions* (not earnings) at any time, penalty-free. This makes a Roth act like an emergency fund backstop.

**You're under the income limit.** For 2026, single filers earning above $161,000 and married couples above $240,000 are phased out of direct Roth contributions.

## Who Should Choose the Traditional IRA?

**You're in a high tax bracket now.** If you're in the 35% bracket today but expect a lower rate in retirement, deferring taxes saves real money.

**You need to reduce your taxable income now.** Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible, lowering your tax bill this year.

**You're closer to retirement.** The fewer years for tax-free growth, the smaller the Roth's advantage.

## The "Tax Diversification" Strategy

Here's the thing most people miss: you don't have to pick just one — at least not forever.

**Contribute to both over your career:**
- Roth in low-income years
- Traditional in high-income years

Having both gives you flexibility in retirement. You can pull from Traditional accounts in years when you're in a low bracket and let the Roth grow untouched.

## The Roth Conversion Ladder

If you already have a large Traditional IRA, consider **converting portions to Roth** in low-income years (e.g., early retirement, a gap year, a year with big deductions). You pay taxes now at a lower rate, then enjoy tax-free growth forever.

## Contribution Limits for 2026

- **Under 50:** $7,000/year
- **50 and older:** $8,000/year (catch-up contribution)

These limits apply across *all* your IRAs combined.

## The Simple Rule of Thumb

- **Expect higher taxes later?** → Roth IRA
- **Expect lower taxes later?** → Traditional IRA
- **Unsure?** → Split it, or go Roth (tax-free income has optionality value)

## Bottom Line

For most people in their 20s–40s, the Roth IRA wins. The tax-free compounding over decades plus the flexibility of no required minimum distributions makes it the more powerful long-term tool. But if you're a high earner today, the Traditional IRA's immediate deduction is hard to ignore.

The best move? Start contributing to *something*, then optimize as your situation becomes clearer. Time in the market beats perfection.
