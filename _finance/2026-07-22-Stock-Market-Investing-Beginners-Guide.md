## What Is the Stock Market, Really?

When you buy a stock, you're buying a small ownership stake in a real company — its earnings, its assets, and its future growth. Stock markets (like the NYSE and NASDAQ) are simply organized exchanges where these ownership stakes are bought and sold.

**The core concept:** Companies need capital to grow. Instead of just borrowing from banks, they sell pieces of themselves (shares) to the public. You profit when the company grows and becomes more valuable.

**Why the market goes up over time:** The global economy, driven by human ingenuity, productivity, and population growth, has expanded for centuries. Owning diversified stocks means owning a piece of that expansion.

## The Power of Compound Growth

Warren Buffett made 99% of his wealth after age 50 — not because he suddenly got smarter, but because of compound growth over time.

**Example:** $10,000 invested at 10% annual return (the historical S&P 500 average):
- After 10 years: $25,937
- After 20 years: $67,275
- After 30 years: $174,494
- After 40 years: $452,593

The money doubles roughly every 7 years. The earlier you start, the more doublings you get.

## What Should a Beginner Buy?

Forget stock-picking. For 99% of people, the answer is simple: **index funds**.

### Index Funds: The Beginner's Best Friend

An index fund is a basket of stocks that tracks a market index, like the S&P 500 (the 500 largest U.S. companies). Instead of betting on one company, you own a slice of 500 companies at once.

**Why index funds win:**
- **Diversification:** One fund = instant ownership in hundreds of companies
- **Low cost:** Expense ratios of 0.03–0.20% vs. 1–2% for managed funds
- **Proven performance:** Over 20-year periods, 80–90% of actively managed funds underperform the S&P 500 index

**The Big Three Index Funds:**

| Fund | Ticker | What It Tracks | Expense Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanguard Total Stock Market | VTI | Entire U.S. market | 0.03% |
| SPDR S&P 500 ETF | SPY | S&P 500 companies | 0.09% |
| Fidelity ZERO Total Market | FZROX | U.S. market, zero cost | 0.00% |

### ETF vs. Mutual Fund

**ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund):** Trades like a stock, can be bought any time during market hours. Minimum investment = price of one share (or fractional shares via most brokers). Great flexibility.

**Mutual Fund:** Trades once per day at close. Often has minimum investment requirements ($1–$3,000 depending on broker). Good for automatic investment plans.

For beginners, ETFs with fractional shares are often the easiest entry point.

## How to Actually Invest: Dollar-Cost Averaging

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** is the strategy of investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule — regardless of whether the market is up or down.

**Example:** Invest $200 every month into VTI
- When price is high, you buy fewer shares
- When price is low, you buy more shares
- Over time, your average cost per share trends lower

**Why DCA beats trying to "time the market":**
- Time in the market beats timing the market — historically, by a large margin
- Removes emotional decision-making
- Builds the investing habit automatically

Most brokerages let you set up automatic investments — use this feature.

## The 5 Biggest Beginner Mistakes

**1. Waiting for the "right time"**
There is no perfect entry point. Every day you wait is a day of compound growth lost. The best time to invest was yesterday; the second-best time is today.

**2. Panic selling during downturns**
Markets decline 10% roughly every 1–2 years and 20%+ every 3–5 years. This is normal. Investors who sell during crashes lock in losses and miss the recovery. Stay the course.

**3. Picking individual stocks**
Selecting individual winners consistently is nearly impossible — even professional fund managers fail at it. Index funds remove this risk.

**4. Ignoring fees**
A 1% annual fee vs. 0.03% on a $100,000 portfolio means paying $970 more per year. Over 30 years, that difference compounds to over $80,000 in lost wealth.

**5. Not investing because "I don't have enough money"**
With fractional shares and no minimums, you can start with $1. The habit matters more than the amount.

## Beginner's 3-Fund Portfolio

If you want a simple, complete portfolio, the classic "three-fund portfolio" covers everything:

1. **U.S. Total Stock Market** (e.g., VTI) — Core growth
2. **International Stocks** (e.g., VXUS) — Global diversification
3. **U.S. Bond Market** (e.g., BND) — Stability

**Sample allocation by age:**
- **20s–30s:** 80% stocks / 20% bonds (aggressive)
- **40s:** 70% stocks / 30% bonds
- **50s+:** 60% stocks / 40% bonds (conservative)

Rebalance once a year to maintain your target allocation.

3. Consistent contributions
