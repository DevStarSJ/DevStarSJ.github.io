For most Americans, **Social Security is the single largest financial asset they'll ever have** — worth hundreds of thousands of dollars in lifetime income. Yet a majority claim it too early, leaving tens of thousands of dollars on the table.

The decision of *when* to claim is one of the most consequential financial choices you'll make in retirement. Here's what you need to know.

## The Basics: When Can You Claim?

Social Security retirement benefits become available at **age 62** — but claiming early means a permanently reduced benefit.

**Key age milestones:**
- **Age 62:** Earliest claiming age. Benefits reduced by up to 30%.
- **Full Retirement Age (FRA):** Age 66–67 depending on birth year. 100% of your earned benefit.
- **Age 70:** Maximum benefit. Benefit increases by 8% per year from FRA to 70.

| Birth Year | Full Retirement Age |
|---|---|
| 1954 | 66 |
| 1955 | 66 + 2 months |
| 1956 | 66 + 4 months |
| 1957 | 66 + 6 months |
| 1958 | 66 + 8 months |
| 1959 | 66 + 10 months |
| 1960+ | 67 |

## The Math: Early vs. Late Claiming

Let's make this concrete. Say your FRA benefit (at 67) would be **$2,000/month**.

| Claim Age | Monthly Benefit | Lifetime Benefit (to age 87) |
|---|---|---|
| 62 | $1,400 (-30%) | $420,000 |
| 65 | $1,733 (-13.3%) | $486,240 |
| 67 (FRA) | $2,000 | $480,000 |
| 70 | $2,480 (+24%) | $446,400 |

**Wait — claiming at 70 yields *less* total than FRA if you live to 87?**

Yes — because you missed 3 years of payments. The **break-even age** for waiting from 67 to 70 is approximately **age 82–83**. If you live past that, you come out ahead by waiting.

The average American who reaches 65 can expect to live to about 85. Many people will live significantly longer.

## The Break-Even Analysis

**Should you claim early or late?** It depends entirely on one thing: **how long you live**.

- **Live shorter than break-even (~82):** Early claiming nets more total dollars
- **Live longer than break-even:** Delayed claiming wins

Since you don't know when you'll die, the practical wisdom is: **if you're healthy, delay. If health is poor, claim earlier.**

Additional factors that push toward *delaying*:
- Family history of longevity
- Good health at 62–67
- Have other income sources to bridge the gap
- Married (survivor benefit strategy — see below)

Additional factors that push toward *early claiming*:
- Poor health or serious illness
- No other retirement income
- Need the money now
- Low break-even confidence

## The Spousal Benefit Multiplier

For married couples, Social Security gets dramatically more strategic.

**Spousal benefit:** A spouse can claim up to **50% of the higher earner's FRA benefit**, even with little work history of their own.

**Survivor benefit:** When one spouse dies, the survivor receives **the higher of the two benefits** — permanently.

### Optimal Strategy for Couples:

The general principle: **the higher earner delays as long as possible; the lower earner claims earlier**.

Here's why: if the higher earner dies first, the survivor receives the higher earner's full benefit for the rest of their life. Delaying that benefit maximizes the survivor's lifetime income.

**Example:**
- High earner: FRA benefit $3,000 → At 70: $3,720/month
- Low earner: FRA benefit $1,200 → Claim at 62: $840/month

If high earner dies at 78, survivor receives $3,720/month for the rest of their life. That's potentially 20+ years of $3,720 vs. $3,000 — a massive difference.

## Working While Collecting Social Security

If you claim before FRA and continue working, your benefits may be temporarily reduced:

**Before FRA:** $1 withheld for every $2 earned above $22,320/year (2026 limit, adjusts annually)

**At/After FRA:** No reduction regardless of earnings.

The good news: withheld benefits aren't lost — they're added back to your monthly benefit after you reach FRA.

## Social Security and Taxes

Social Security benefits may be **taxable** depending on your total income:

| Combined Income | Taxable Amount |
|---|---|
| Single < $25,000 | 0% taxable |
| Single $25,000–$34,000 | Up to 50% taxable |
| Single > $34,000 | Up to 85% taxable |
| Married < $32,000 | 0% taxable |
| Married $32,000–$44,000 | Up to 50% taxable |
| Married > $44,000 | Up to 85% taxable |

Note: "Combined income" = AGI + nontaxable interest + 50% of SS benefits.

**Tax strategy:** Roth conversions before claiming SS can reduce taxable income in those early retirement years, potentially keeping SS benefits in a lower tax bracket.

## 5 Social Security Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Claiming at 62 by default** — "I'll take it before they cut it." The system has problems but will likely remain. Don't sacrifice 30% of benefits on this fear.

2. **Forgetting about the survivor benefit** — This affects your spouse's income for decades after you're gone. The higher-earner delaying is often a gift to your spouse.

3. **Not checking your earnings record** — Errors on your SSA record directly reduce your benefit. Review your record at ssa.gov every few years.

4. **Ignoring the tax implications** — Claiming at 62 while still working can mean high taxes on benefits plus reduced benefits. Know the math.

5. **Treating it in isolation** — Social Security should integrate with your broader retirement plan: 401(k) withdrawals, Roth conversions, pension income, Medicare timing.

## The Bottom Line

The difference between claiming at 62 and 70 can exceed **$200,000+ in lifetime benefits** for someone with a healthy lifespan. This is not a small decision.

The default shouldn't be "claim as soon as possible." The default should be: **build a bridge income strategy that lets you delay at least to FRA, and if possible to 70**.

What's your bridge? That's the real retirement planning question.
