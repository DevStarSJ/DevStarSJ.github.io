Choosing between a **Roth IRA** and a **Traditional IRA** is one of the most important retirement planning decisions you'll make. Both accounts offer powerful tax advantages, but they work in fundamentally different ways. The right choice depends on your current tax bracket, expected future income, and retirement timeline.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay Taxes

This is everything:

- **Traditional IRA:** Tax deduction now → Pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement
- **Roth IRA:** Pay taxes now → Tax-free withdrawals in retirement

Both accounts grow **tax-free** while invested. The only difference is *when* you pay the IRS.

## 2026 Contribution Limits

| | Both IRA Types |
|---|---|
| Under 50 | $7,000/year |
| Age 50+ | $8,000/year (catch-up) |

**Important:** This $7,000 limit is combined across ALL your IRAs. If you contribute $4,000 to a Roth IRA, you can only put $3,000 in a Traditional IRA.

## Roth IRA: Details

### Income Limits (2026)
You can only contribute to a Roth IRA if your income is below:

| Filing Status | Full Contribution | Phase-out | No Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | Under $150,000 | $150K–$165K | Over $165,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | Under $236,000 | $236K–$246K | Over $246,000 |

### Key Benefits:
✅ **Tax-free growth AND withdrawals** in retirement  
✅ **No Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)** — money grows forever  
✅ **Contributions can be withdrawn anytime** penalty-free (not earnings)  
✅ **Flexible for estate planning** — heirs can inherit tax-free  
✅ Great hedge against **future tax rate increases**  

### Drawbacks:
❌ No upfront tax deduction  
❌ Income limits exclude high earners  
❌ Five-year rule applies to earnings withdrawals  

## Traditional IRA: Details

### Who Can Deduct Contributions
If you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan (401k), the deduction phases out at:

| Filing Status | Full Deduction | Phase-out | No Deduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single (has 401k) | Under $79,000 | $79K–$89K | Over $89,000 |
| Married (has 401k) | Under $126,000 | $126K–$146K | Over $146,000 |

**No workplace plan?** You can always deduct regardless of income.

### Key Benefits:
✅ **Immediate tax deduction** reduces taxable income today  
✅ **No income limits** to contribute (just to deduct)  
✅ Beneficial if you expect a **lower tax rate in retirement**  
✅ Can convert to Roth later (Roth conversion ladder)  

### Drawbacks:
❌ Withdrawals taxed as ordinary income  
❌ **Required Minimum Distributions starting at age 73**  
❌ 10% penalty for early withdrawals (before 59½)  
❌ More complex tax planning required  

## The Big Question: Will Taxes Be Higher or Lower in Retirement?

This is the central decision point:

**Choose Roth IRA if:**
- You're in a **low tax bracket now** (early career, low income years)
- You expect **higher income/taxes in retirement**
- You want **flexibility** (no RMDs, contribution access)
- You're under 40 with decades of tax-free growth ahead
- Tax rates will likely **increase** in the future (a real possibility)

**Choose Traditional IRA if:**
- You're in a **high tax bracket now**
- You expect **lower income in retirement** (most retirees)
- You need the **immediate tax deduction** to reduce current taxes
- You'll benefit from the lower tax bracket in retirement
- You're close to retirement and want to reduce taxable income now

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Factor | Roth IRA | Traditional IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Tax Benefit | Tax-free withdrawals | Tax deduction now |
| Contribution Age | Any age | Any age |
| Income Limits | Yes | No (deductibility has limits) |
| RMDs | No | Yes (age 73) |
| Early Withdrawal | Contributions OK; 10% on earnings | 10% penalty |
| Best For | Young/low-income investors | High-income, near-retirement |

## The Backdoor Roth IRA (For High Earners)

If you earn too much for a direct Roth IRA contribution, use the **Backdoor Roth IRA strategy**:

1. Contribute to a **non-deductible Traditional IRA** (no income limits)
2. Convert it to a Roth IRA immediately
3. Pay taxes only on any gains (usually minimal if converted quickly)

This is legal and widely used by high earners. Consult a tax professional for the pro-rata rule considerations.

## Real-World Example

**Scenario:** 30-year-old earning $60,000, invests $7,000/year for 35 years, 8% average return

| | Roth IRA | Traditional IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Account value at 65 | $1,147,000 | $1,147,000 |
| Tax rate at withdrawal (22%) | $0 | $252,340 taxes paid |
| **After-tax value** | **$1,147,000** | **$894,660** |

**Roth wins by $252,340** in this scenario — because this investor was young and in a lower tax bracket during contributions.

## Can You Have Both?

**Yes!** You can contribute to both a Roth and Traditional IRA in the same year, as long as your **combined contributions don't exceed $7,000** ($8,000 if 50+).

Many financial advisors recommend **tax diversification** — having both types of accounts to hedge against uncertain future tax rates.

## The 401(k) + IRA Strategy

If your employer offers a 401(k), consider this order:
1. **Contribute to 401(k) up to the employer match** (free money first!)
2. **Max out Roth IRA** ($7,000)
3. **Go back and max 401(k)** ($23,500 in 2026)

## Bottom Line

Neither Roth nor Traditional IRA is universally better. The right choice depends on your current tax rate vs. expected retirement tax rate.

**Quick rule of thumb:**
- **Young and low-income → Roth IRA**
- **High-income and close to retirement → Traditional IRA**
- **Unsure? → Split between both for tax diversification**

Start contributing now. Even a "wrong" choice between Roth and Traditional still puts you miles ahead of someone who never invests at all.
