# Retirement Savings by Age: How Much Should You Have Saved?

One of the most common personal finance questions is also one of the most anxiety-inducing: **"Am I saving enough for retirement?"**

The honest answer depends on your income, spending habits, retirement goals, and timeline. But general benchmarks can help you gauge where you stand — and what to do if you're falling behind.

Here's a comprehensive guide to retirement savings targets at every stage of life, plus practical steps to catch up if needed.

## The Standard Benchmark: Fidelity's Guidelines

Fidelity Investments offers widely-cited retirement savings benchmarks based on your salary:

| Age | Savings Target |
|-----|---------------|
| 30 | 1× your annual salary |
| 35 | 2× your annual salary |
| 40 | 3× your annual salary |
| 45 | 4× your annual salary |
| 50 | 6× your annual salary |
| 55 | 7× your annual salary |
| 60 | 8× your annual salary |
| 67 (retirement) | 10× your annual salary |

**Example:** If you earn $80,000/year, by age 40 you should have approximately $240,000 saved for retirement.

These assume you:
- Save 15% of income annually (including employer match)
- Invest in a balanced portfolio
- Retire at 67
- Spend 45% of pre-retirement income in retirement

---

## The 25× Rule (FIRE Method)

A more precise calculation: **save 25× your expected annual retirement expenses**.

This comes from the **4% Safe Withdrawal Rate** — the idea that you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio annually and it will last 30+ years.

### How to Calculate Your Number

1. Estimate your annual retirement spending
2. Multiply by 25

Examples:
| Annual Retirement Spending | Retirement Target |
|--------------------------|------------------|
| $30,000/year | **$750,000** |
| $50,000/year | **$1,250,000** |
| $80,000/year | **$2,000,000** |
| $100,000/year | **$2,500,000** |

This is your **FIRE number** — the portfolio size where you're financially independent.

## Retirement Savings Benchmarks by Age (Detailed)

### In Your 20s

**Target by 25:** 0.5× salary
**Target by 30:** 1× salary

Your 20s are about **building habits**, not hitting targets. The most important action:
- Open a 401(k) and contribute enough to get the full employer match (free money!)
- Open a Roth IRA (you're likely in a lower tax bracket now — tax-free growth forever)
- Invest in low-cost index funds

Even if you can only save $100/month at 22, it grows dramatically:
- $100/month at 22, 8% return → **$357,000 by 65**
- $100/month at 32, 8% return → **$163,000 by 65**

Don't underestimate small amounts when time is on your side.

---

### In Your 30s

**Target by 35:** 2× salary
**Target by 40:** 3× salary

The 30s are often squeeze years — buying homes, raising kids, paying down debt. But they're also peak opportunity:

- Increase your savings rate to **15%+ of gross income**
- Don't stop 401(k) contributions for "temporary" needs
- Reevaluate your asset allocation: still aggressive (80%+ stocks)
- If behind: cut one major expense and redirect to retirement savings

---

### In Your 40s

**Target by 45:** 4× salary
**Target by 50:** 6× salary

If you're behind, this is the decade to get serious. Strategies:

**Maximize contributions:**
- 401(k) max: $23,500/year in 2026
- Roth IRA max: $7,000/year
- HSA: $4,300/year (triple tax advantage — contributes to healthcare costs in retirement)

**Asset allocation:** 70–80% stocks, 20–30% bonds. You still have 20+ years.

**Side hustle:** Extra income in your 40s, invested entirely, can close a large gap.

---

### In Your 50s

**Target by 55:** 7× salary
**Target by 60:** 8× salary

Catch-up contributions kick in:
- 401(k): **$31,000/year** (regular $23,500 + $7,500 catch-up)
- Roth IRA: **$8,000/year** ($7,000 + $1,000 catch-up)
- HSA: **$5,150/year** ($4,300 + $850 catch-up)

**Key 50s moves:**
- Pay off mortgage if possible (eliminates biggest expense in retirement)
- Gradually shift to more conservative allocation (60–70% stocks)
- Understand your Social Security benefit options
- Consider part-time work post-retirement to bridge income gap

---

### In Your 60s

**Target by 60:** 8× salary
**Target at 67:** 10× salary

**Pre-retirement checklist:**
- [ ] Know your Social Security benefit at different claim ages
- [ ] Model different withdrawal strategies (Roth vs. traditional)
- [ ] Understand Required Minimum Distributions (start at 73)
- [ ] Evaluate healthcare coverage until Medicare eligibility (65)
- [ ] Have 1–2 years of cash reserves to avoid selling in downturns

## What If You're Behind?

Don't panic. **Late starters can still retire well** — it just requires more aggressive action.

### Catch-Up Strategies

**1. Increase Your Savings Rate Dramatically**
- Every 1% increase in savings rate translates to meaningful improvement
- Cut the top 3 expenses: housing, car, dining
- Redirect raises and bonuses 100% to retirement accounts

**2. Work Longer (Even 1–2 Years Makes a Difference)**
- Working to 68 instead of 66: 2 more years of saving + 2 fewer years of spending + higher Social Security
- This can change your retirement picture dramatically

**3. Delay Social Security**
- Claiming at 62: Reduced benefit (permanently)
- Claiming at 67: Full benefit
- Claiming at 70: **132% of full benefit** — the highest possible

Delaying Social Security from 62 to 70 can increase your monthly payment by **75%**.

**4. Reduce Retirement Expenses**
- Retire to a lower cost-of-living area
- Downsize housing
- Plan to have no mortgage in retirement
- Each $1,000 less in annual expenses = $25,000 less you need saved

**5. Consider Part-Time Work**
- Working 20 hours/week earning $25,000/year reduces portfolio withdrawals significantly
- This "semi-retirement" can make retirement sustainable even with smaller savings

## The Social Security Factor

Many retirement calculations forget Social Security. In 2026:

- Average Social Security benefit: **~$1,907/month**
- Maximum benefit at 70: **~$4,873/month**

For a couple, combined Social Security of $3,000–$6,000/month significantly reduces how much you need saved.

If your investments + Social Security cover 100% of expected expenses, you're on track regardless of the absolute portfolio size.

## The Right Accounts (And the Right Order)

![Financial planning documents and calculator](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1554224154-26032ffc0d07?w=800)
*Photo by [Precondo CA](https://unsplash.com/@precondo) on Unsplash*

### Optimal Contribution Order in 2026

1. **401(k)** up to employer match (100% return from free money)
2. **HSA** max ($4,300/year) — triple tax advantage
3. **Roth IRA** max ($7,000/year)
4. **401(k)** max remaining ($23,500 total)
5. **Taxable brokerage** for any additional savings

### Roth vs. Traditional — Which to Choose?

- **Roth IRA:** Pay taxes now, withdraw tax-free later. Best if you're in a lower tax bracket now.
- **Traditional 401(k)/IRA:** Pay taxes later. Best if in a higher bracket now.

For most people under 50: prioritize **Roth** for tax-free retirement income.

## Final Thoughts

The benchmarks exist to guide, not to judge. Whether you're ahead, on track, or behind, the most important thing is to **know your number and make a plan**.

The power of compound interest means even modest action taken today has dramatic long-term effects. The second-best time to start planning for retirement is always right now.

Look at your current balance. Compare it to the benchmark. If there's a gap, pick one thing to change this month. Then another next month.

Retirement is not a luxury — it's the natural end of a well-lived financial life. Build toward it with intention.
