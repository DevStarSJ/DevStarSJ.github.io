The U.S. tax code is full of loopholes that favor the wealthy — but there are two that anyone can use: the **401(k)** and the **IRA**. Together, they can shelter tens of thousands of dollars from taxes every year, compounding your wealth dramatically faster than a standard taxable account. Here's exactly how to use them.

## Why Tax-Advantaged Accounts Are a Superpower

Consider two investors, each putting away $10,000/year at a 7% return for 30 years:

- **Taxable account** (25% tax drag on dividends/gains): ~$720,000
- **Tax-advantaged account** (no annual tax drag): ~$944,000

**The difference: $224,000 — just from taxes.** That's the power of these accounts. Now let's learn how to max them out.

## Part 1: The 401(k) — Your Employer-Sponsored Plan

### What Is a 401(k)?
A 401(k) is a retirement savings plan offered by employers. Your contributions come directly from your paycheck before taxes, reducing your taxable income today.

### 2026 Contribution Limits
- **Employee contribution:** $23,500/year
- **Catch-up (age 50+):** Additional $7,500 → total $31,000
- **Employer match:** Doesn't count toward your limit (total combined cap: $70,000)

### Traditional vs. Roth 401(k)

| | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributions** | Pre-tax (reduces today's income) | After-tax |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals** | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free (in retirement) |
| **Best for** | High earners now, lower tax bracket in retirement | Lower earners now, higher bracket expected later |

### Step-by-Step: Maximizing Your 401(k)

**Step 1: Get the full employer match — always**
If your employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, contribute at least 6%. Not doing so is leaving free money on the table — it's an instant 50% return.

**Step 2: Increase contributions by 1% per raise**
Every time you get a raise, bump your 401(k) contribution by 1%. You won't miss money you never saw in your paycheck.

**Step 3: Aim to max out ($23,500)**
On a $75,000 salary, maxing out requires contributing ~31% of income. Many people get there gradually over several years.

**Step 4: Choose your investments wisely**
Most plans offer index funds. Look for low-cost options (expense ratio under 0.2%):
- Target-date fund (e.g., "2050 Fund") — automatic asset allocation
- S&P 500 index fund — core US equity exposure
- Total market index fund — broader diversification

## Part 2: The IRA — Your Personal Retirement Account

### What Is an IRA?
An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a personal retirement account you open yourself — not tied to an employer.

### 2026 Contribution Limits
- **Annual limit:** $7,000/year ($8,000 if age 50+)
- **Deadline:** April 15 of the following tax year

### Traditional IRA vs. Roth IRA

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Tax deduction** | Yes (if income qualifies) | No |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals** | Taxed as income | Tax-free after age 59½ |
| **RMDs** | Required starting at 73 | None during owner's lifetime |
| **Income limits** | None to contribute (deductibility phases out) | Phases out above ~$161,000 (single) |

### The Roth IRA: Why It's a Fan Favorite

For most people under 40, the Roth IRA is the gold standard. Your contributions grow tax-free for decades, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free — including all the earnings.

**Extra benefit:** Roth contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn penalty-free at any time, making it a flexible emergency backup.

### The Backdoor Roth IRA (For High Earners)
If your income exceeds Roth IRA limits, you can:
1. Contribute to a Traditional IRA (no income limit for contributions)
2. Convert it to a Roth IRA immediately ("backdoor conversion")

This is legal and widely used by high earners.

## The Optimal Order: How to Prioritize

When you have limited extra money, use this priority order:

1. **401(k) to get full employer match** (free money — always first)
2. **Roth IRA to max** ($7,000/year) — tax-free growth is valuable
3. **401(k) to the annual max** ($23,500) — shelter more from taxes
4. **HSA if eligible** ($4,300 single / $8,550 family in 2026) — triple tax advantage
5. **Taxable brokerage** — no limits, maximum flexibility

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Leaving employer match on the table:** Every dollar of employer match you miss is gone forever. Prioritize this above everything.

**Investing too conservatively:** A 35-year-old with 30 years until retirement should be mostly in stocks. Bonds provide stability but limit growth over long time horizons.

**Cashing out when changing jobs:** Rolling over your 401(k) to an IRA or new employer plan preserves tax advantages. Cashing out triggers taxes + 10% penalty.

**Not increasing contributions over time:** Set an annual reminder to review and increase contributions, especially after raises.

**Waiting until you "can afford it":** Starting at 25 vs. 35 with the same contribution can result in **two to three times more money** at retirement due to compounding. Time is your most valuable asset.

## What Maxing Out Actually Looks Like

Full 401(k) + Roth IRA in 2026:
- **Total annual contribution:** $23,500 + $7,000 = **$30,500**
- At 7% return over 30 years: **~$3,000,000**

Even contributing half that amount — $15,250/year — grows to over $1.4 million in 30 years.

The goal isn't perfection. It's consistency. Start where you are, increase every year, and let time do the heavy lifting.
