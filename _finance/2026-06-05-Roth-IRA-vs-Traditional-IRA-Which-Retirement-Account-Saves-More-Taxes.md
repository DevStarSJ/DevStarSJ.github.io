The difference between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA might seem like a technicality — but it could mean tens of thousands of dollars more (or less) in retirement. The decision comes down to a single question: **do you want to pay taxes now, or later?**

Both accounts let your investments grow tax-advantaged. The distinction is *when* the IRS takes its cut.

## The Core Difference: Tax Timing

**Traditional IRA — Pay taxes later**
- Contributions may be **tax-deductible** (reduces this year's taxable income)
- Money grows tax-deferred
- You pay income taxes **when you withdraw** in retirement

**Roth IRA — Pay taxes now**
- Contributions are **after-tax** (no deduction this year)
- Money grows **tax-free**
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are **completely tax-free**

Think of it this way: Traditional IRA is a "pay later" deal. Roth IRA is a "pay now, never again" deal.

## 2026 Contribution Limits

Both accounts share the same annual contribution limit:
- **Under 50:** $7,000/year
- **Age 50+:** $8,000/year (catch-up contribution)

You can split contributions between both accounts — but the total cannot exceed the limit. ($4,000 Roth + $3,000 Traditional = $7,000 total ✅)

**Roth IRA income limits for 2026:**
- Single filers: Full contribution up to $146,000 AGI; phase-out to $161,000
- Married filing jointly: Full contribution up to $230,000; phase-out to $240,000
- Above the phase-out: Cannot contribute directly (but "backdoor Roth" is possible)

Traditional IRA has no income limits for contributions — but deductibility phases out at higher incomes if you have a workplace retirement plan.

## The Math: Which Wins Over 30 Years?

Let's run the numbers for a 30-year-old investing $7,000/year until 65, with 8% annual returns:

**Assumptions:**
- Current tax rate: 22%
- Retirement tax rate: 22% (same)
- Investment: $7,000/year for 35 years

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Annual contribution | $7,000 (pre-tax deduction saves $1,540 now) | $7,000 (after-tax, no deduction) |
| Total at 65 (8% return) | $1,374,000 | $1,374,000 |
| Taxes owed at withdrawal (22%) | -$302,000 | $0 |
| **After-tax balance** | **$1,072,000** | **$1,374,000** |

In this scenario with the same tax rate, **Roth wins by $302,000** — because those 35 years of gains are never taxed.

But the calculus shifts based on tax rates:

- **If retirement tax rate is LOWER** than current → Traditional wins
- **If retirement tax rate is HIGHER** than current → Roth wins
- **If tax rates are the same** → Roth typically wins (due to tax-free growth on earnings)

## Key Decision Factors

### 1. Current vs Expected Future Tax Rate

This is the primary factor:

**Lean Roth if:**
- You're young and early in your career (taxes likely higher later)
- You expect your income to grow significantly
- You believe tax rates will rise in the future
- You're in the 12% or 22% bracket now

**Lean Traditional if:**
- You're in your peak earning years (high tax rate now)
- You expect retirement income to be modest
- You're in the 32%+ bracket and will likely drop significantly in retirement

### 2. Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

Traditional IRA requires you to start withdrawing at age 73 (RMD rules) — whether you need the money or not. These mandatory withdrawals can push you into higher tax brackets.

Roth IRA has **no RMDs during your lifetime**. This makes it excellent for:
- Estate planning (pass tax-free wealth to heirs)
- Delaying withdrawals if you have other income sources
- Flexible retirement income management

### 3. Flexibility and Emergency Access

Roth IRA allows you to **withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time, penalty-free**. This makes it function partly as an accessible savings account.

Traditional IRA withdrawals before 59½ incur a 10% penalty plus taxes.

This flexibility is a significant advantage of Roth — it's like having a tax-advantaged retirement account AND an accessible backup fund.

### 4. The "Backdoor Roth" Strategy

High earners above the Roth income limits can still access a Roth IRA through the "backdoor" method:
1. Contribute to a non-deductible Traditional IRA (no income limit)
2. Convert it to a Roth IRA
3. Pay taxes on any pre-tax funds converted

This is perfectly legal and widely used by high-income earners.

## What About the 401(k)?

Your workplace 401(k) is separate and often comes in two flavors too: Traditional 401(k) and Roth 401(k). The same tax-now vs. tax-later logic applies.

**Smart sequencing for most people:**
1. **401(k) up to employer match** (free money — always first)
2. **Max out Roth IRA** ($7,000)
3. **Return to 401(k)** to max it out ($23,500 in 2026)
4. **Taxable brokerage account** for anything beyond

## Quick Decision Framework

**Choose Roth IRA if you:**
- Are under 40 and/or in a lower tax bracket
- Want tax-free income in retirement
- Want flexibility to access contributions penalty-free
- Expect your tax rate to rise
- Are interested in leaving tax-free inheritance
- Are a high earner who can do the backdoor Roth

**Choose Traditional IRA if you:**
- Are in peak earning years with high current tax rate
- Expect significantly lower income in retirement
- Want to reduce your taxable income this year
- Are above Roth income limits without the backdoor option

**Do both if:**
- You're unsure about future tax rates (diversify your tax exposure)
- You want flexibility in how you generate retirement income

## The Real Answer

For most people under 40, **the Roth IRA is the default choice**. The combination of tax-free growth, no RMDs, contribution withdrawal flexibility, and the likelihood of higher future income (and thus higher tax rates) makes it the better long-term bet.

That said, a Traditional IRA is genuinely better for high earners in peak years who need the tax deduction now. The choice isn't always obvious — which is why "do both" and diversify your tax exposure is often the wisest answer.

The most important thing? Start contributing — to *either* account — as early as possible. The difference between Roth and Traditional pales in comparison to the difference between starting at 25 vs. 35.
