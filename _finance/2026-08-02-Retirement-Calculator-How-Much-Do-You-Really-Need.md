"How much do I need to retire?"

It's the question behind every financial decision you make. And for most people, the answer is some vague, intimidating number — "a few million" — without a real plan to get there.

Let's fix that. Here's exactly how to calculate your retirement number and build a concrete path to reach it.

## The 4% Rule: Your Foundation

The 4% rule is the most widely used framework for retirement planning. It comes from the Trinity Study (1998), which analyzed historical market data to determine a "safe withdrawal rate."

The finding: **You can withdraw 4% of your portfolio each year in retirement and have a very high probability your money lasts 30+ years.**

This means:
- Annual expenses × 25 = Your retirement number

**Examples:**

| Annual Expenses | Retirement Number (25×) |
|----------------|------------------------|
| $30,000 | $750,000 |
| $50,000 | $1,250,000 |
| $80,000 | $2,000,000 |
| $100,000 | $2,500,000 |

If you can live on $50,000/year, you need $1.25 million. Not $10 million. Not even $5 million.

## Calculating Your Personal Retirement Number

**Step 1: Determine your annual retirement expenses**

Think through:
- Housing (mortgage paid off? downsizing?)
- Food and utilities
- Healthcare (biggest variable for pre-Medicare retirees)
- Travel and leisure
- Insurance

Most people spend 70–80% of their pre-retirement income in retirement. Use 80–90% for a conservative estimate.

**Step 2: Account for Social Security / Pension**

If you'll receive Social Security or a pension, subtract that from your annual need before multiplying by 25.

Example: Need $60,000/year. Expect $18,000 from Social Security.  
Remaining need: $42,000/year → Retirement number: **$1,050,000**

**Step 3: Multiply by 25**

Annual expenses (after subtracting guaranteed income) × 25 = Your number.

## How Long Will It Take?

The time to reach your retirement number depends on:
1. How much you currently have saved
2. How much you save each month
3. Your expected rate of return

A rough guide using the rule of 72:

> At 7% average annual return, money doubles every **~10 years**.

With consistent investing, time is your greatest asset:

| Monthly Investment | Years to $1M (7% return) |
|-------------------|--------------------------|
| $500 | 36 years |
| $1,000 | 29 years |
| $2,000 | 23 years |
| $3,000 | 19 years |

Starting at 25 vs. 35 can make a difference of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

## Where to Put Retirement Savings

**Priority order:**

1. **401(k) up to employer match** — Free money. Always take the full match first.
2. **HSA (if eligible)** — Triple tax advantaged: contributions deductible, growth tax-free, withdrawals for medical expenses tax-free
3. **Roth IRA to max** — Tax-free growth and withdrawals; $7,000 limit in 2026 (under 50)
4. **401(k) to max** — $23,500 limit in 2026 (under 50)
5. **Taxable brokerage** — For savings beyond tax-advantaged limits

## Strategies to Reach Your Number Faster

### Increase Your Savings Rate

The single most powerful lever. Each 1% increase in savings rate can reduce your working years significantly.

| Savings Rate | Years to Retirement (starting from zero) |
|-------------|------------------------------------------|
| 10% | ~43 years |
| 20% | ~37 years |
| 30% | ~28 years |
| 50% | ~17 years |
| 70% | ~8 years |

*(Assumes 5% real return, spending all remaining income)*

### Maximize Your Earning Power

Investing in your career — certifications, skills, negotiating raises — can increase the capital available to invest more than almost anything else.

### Control Your Lifestyle Inflation

Every dollar of lifestyle you add now is a dollar that will cost you 25× more to maintain in retirement. Keeping expenses low while earning more is the fastest path.

### Consider a Roth Conversion Ladder

If you retire early (before 59½), a Roth conversion ladder lets you access retirement funds penalty-free by converting traditional IRA/401k funds to Roth gradually and withdrawing contributions after 5 years.

## Common Retirement Planning Mistakes

**Starting too late:** Waiting until 40 to start investing seriously means missing the most powerful compounding years. Start now, even if the amount is small.

**Underestimating healthcare costs:** For early retirees, healthcare can cost $12,000–$24,000/year before Medicare eligibility at 65. Budget for it explicitly.

**Forgetting inflation:** Your $1.25M retirement number needs to be reached *in today's dollars*. Factor in that prices will be higher when you actually retire.

**Withdrawing during downturns:** The "sequence of returns risk" means withdrawing during a market crash can permanently impair your portfolio. Keep 1–2 years of expenses in cash or bonds as a buffer.

## Is $1 Million Enough?

For many people — yes. Especially if you:
- Have Social Security income
- Have paid off your home
- Can live on $40,000–$50,000/year
- Are flexible about part-time work in early retirement

For people with higher lifestyle expenses or retiring very early (40s), more capital and/or lower withdrawal rates (3–3.5%) provide more security.

## Bottom Line

Your retirement number is not some mysterious figure. It's your annual expenses multiplied by 25. Once you know it, you can build a clear plan to reach it — with consistent investing, time, and strategic account choices.

The hardest step is starting. The second hardest is staying the course for decades. Do both, and retirement becomes inevitable.
