# Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA: Which Retirement Account Will Save You More Money?

If there's one financial decision that has an outsized impact on your retirement, it's this one: which type of IRA to open. Make the right call and you could save tens of thousands in taxes over your lifetime. Make the wrong one and you'll pay more than you should.

The good news: the decision isn't that complicated once you understand the key differences.

## What Is an IRA?

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a special type of investment account with significant tax advantages designed to encourage long-term retirement savings. You can hold stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds, and other investments inside an IRA — the "account" is just a tax wrapper, not an investment itself.

There are two main types: **Traditional IRA** and **Roth IRA**. The core difference between them comes down to *when* you pay taxes.

## Traditional IRA: Tax Deferred

### How It Works
- **Contributions:** May be tax-deductible (reduces your taxable income now)
- **Growth:** Tax-deferred (you don't pay taxes on dividends, interest, or gains each year)
- **Withdrawals:** Taxed as ordinary income in retirement

### The Tax Advantage
If you contribute $6,500 in a year when you're in the 24% tax bracket, you could reduce your tax bill by **$1,560** that year. The money then grows tax-deferred for decades.

### Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)
At age 73, you **must** start withdrawing a minimum amount each year (called RMDs), whether you need the money or not. This can push you into higher tax brackets and complicates estate planning.

### When Traditional IRA Makes Sense
- You expect to be in a **lower tax bracket in retirement** than you are now
- You want the tax deduction today to invest the savings
- You're in a high income year and want to lower your taxable income
- You're close to retirement and expect lower income soon

## Roth IRA: Tax-Free Growth

### How It Works
- **Contributions:** Made with after-tax money (no deduction now)
- **Growth:** Completely tax-free
- **Withdrawals:** Tax-free in retirement (including all the growth!)

### The Tax Advantage
You pay taxes on the seed, not the harvest. A $6,500 contribution made at age 25, growing at 7% annually, becomes approximately **$98,000 by age 65** — and you owe **zero taxes** on that $91,500 in growth.

### No RMDs
Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions during your lifetime. Your money can compound indefinitely, which is a massive estate planning advantage.

### When Roth IRA Makes Sense
- You're in a **lower tax bracket now** than you expect in retirement
- You're young (more years of tax-free compounding)
- You expect tax rates to be higher in the future
- You want tax diversification in retirement
- You might need flexibility (Roth contributions — not earnings — can be withdrawn tax-free at any time)

## 2026 IRA Contribution Limits

| Account | Under Age 50 | Age 50+ (Catch-Up) |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional IRA | $7,000 | $8,000 |
| Roth IRA | $7,000 | $8,000 |

**Combined limit:** You can contribute to both types, but your total contributions across all IRAs cannot exceed the annual limit ($7,000 or $8,000).

### Roth IRA Income Limits (2026)

Roth IRA contributions are phased out at higher incomes:

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Begins | Phase-Out Ends (No Contribution) |
|---|---|---|
| Single | ~$150,000 | ~$165,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | ~$236,000 | ~$246,000 |

If you earn too much for a Roth IRA, there's a workaround (covered below).

Traditional IRA has no income limit for contributions, but deductibility phases out if you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan.

## Head-to-Head Comparison

| Feature | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Tax on Contributions | Deductible (often) | After-tax |
| Tax on Growth | Deferred | Tax-free |
| Tax on Withdrawals | Taxed as income | Tax-free |
| Required Minimum Distributions | Yes, at age 73 | No |
| Income Limits | None (deductibility limited) | Yes (~$165K single) |
| Early Withdrawal Penalty | 10% + taxes before 59½ | 10% on earnings before 59½ |
| Best For | Higher income now, lower in retirement | Lower income now, higher in retirement |

## The Strategy Most People Miss: Tax Diversification

The honest answer to "Roth vs Traditional?" is often: **both**.

In retirement, having both types of accounts gives you enormous flexibility:
- In a low-income year, withdraw from Traditional (taxed at a lower rate)
- In a high-income year, withdraw from Roth (tax-free)
- Minimize RMDs from Traditional by converting some to Roth before age 73

**Roth conversions:** In years when your income is lower than usual (job change, taking time off, early retirement), you can convert Traditional IRA funds to a Roth IRA. You pay taxes on the converted amount at that year's lower rate, then all future growth is tax-free.

## The Backdoor Roth IRA (For High Earners)

If your income exceeds the Roth IRA limit, you can still access Roth tax advantages through the "backdoor Roth IRA":

1. Make a **non-deductible contribution** to a Traditional IRA (no income limit)
2. Convert that Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA

Since you already paid taxes on the contribution, there's minimal tax impact. The money then grows tax-free in the Roth.

**Note:** This strategy works cleanly if you have no other Traditional IRA funds. If you do, the "pro-rata rule" may create complications — consult a tax professional.

## Practical Decision Guide

**Choose Roth IRA if:**
✅ You're under 40 (more compounding time)
✅ Your income is below $100,000
✅ You're in the 22% tax bracket or lower
✅ You expect to be in a higher bracket in retirement
✅ You want flexibility (no RMDs, contribution access)

**Choose Traditional IRA if:**
✅ You're in the 32% tax bracket or higher
✅ You want to reduce your taxable income this year
✅ You'll clearly be in a lower bracket in retirement
✅ You're close to retirement and want the deduction now

**Do both if:**
✅ You want tax diversification
✅ Your income is in the middle ranges
✅ You're uncertain about future tax rates
✅ You maxed out one and have more to contribute

## Getting Started

1. **Open an IRA** at a major brokerage (look for no account fees, large fund selection, good interface)
2. **Choose your account type** based on the guidance above
3. **Set up automatic contributions** — $583/month maxes the annual limit
4. **Invest in a diversified portfolio** — target date funds or broad market index funds work well for most people
5. **Don't touch it** — the only bad IRA strategy is withdrawing early

## The Bottom Line

Both Traditional and Roth IRAs are powerful tools. The "best" one depends on your current tax bracket versus your expected retirement bracket — and that's a prediction nobody can make perfectly.

When in doubt: **favor the Roth when young**, consider Traditional as income grows, and use both for tax diversification as your net worth increases.

The biggest mistake isn't choosing the "wrong" type — it's not having either one. Open the account first. Optimize later.

**Every year you wait to open an IRA is a year of tax-free compounding you'll never get back.**
