When it comes to retirement savings, choosing between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA isn't just a tax question — it's a life question. The right answer depends on your income, your tax situation now versus in retirement, and your overall financial plan.

Let's break down exactly how each works, who benefits most, and how to make the decision with confidence.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay Taxes

Both accounts grow **tax-free** inside — no capital gains taxes, no dividend taxes while the money is invested. The key difference is *when* you pay income taxes:

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributions** | Pre-tax (tax deductible) | After-tax (no deduction) |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals in retirement** | Taxed as ordinary income | **Tax-free** |
| **Required Minimum Distributions** | Yes (starting at age 73) | No |
| **Early withdrawal penalty** | Yes (before 59½) | Contributions only: No |

## 2026 IRA Contribution Limits

- **Under 50:** $7,000/year
- **Age 50+:** $8,000/year (catch-up contribution)

You can contribute to both a Traditional and Roth IRA in the same year, but the **combined total cannot exceed the limit**.

## Roth IRA Income Limits (2026)

To contribute directly to a Roth IRA, your income must be below:
- **Single filers:** Full contribution up to $150,000 MAGI; phases out at $165,000
- **Married filing jointly:** Full contribution up to $236,000 MAGI; phases out at $246,000

*Above these limits? You can still use the "Backdoor Roth IRA" strategy.*

Traditional IRA contributions have **no income limit**, but tax deductibility phases out if you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan.

## The Roth IRA Advantage

### 1. Tax-Free Retirement Income
Every dollar you withdraw in retirement is 100% yours — no federal income taxes. This is massive if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement.

### 2. No Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)
Traditional IRAs force you to start withdrawing at age 73 (whether you want to or not). Roth IRAs have no such requirement — your money can compound indefinitely.

### 3. Flexibility Before Retirement
You can withdraw your **contributions** (not earnings) from a Roth IRA at any time, penalty-free. This makes it a hybrid emergency fund + retirement account.

### 4. Estate Planning Benefits
Roth IRAs are excellent inheritance vehicles — heirs receive tax-free distributions.

## The Traditional IRA Advantage

### 1. Tax Break Today
If you're in a high tax bracket *now*, deducting contributions reduces your taxable income immediately. A $7,000 contribution in the 24% bracket saves $1,680 in taxes this year.

### 2. Lower Tax Rate in Retirement
If you expect to be in a lower tax bracket when you retire (common for high earners), paying taxes later (at the lower rate) beats paying taxes now.

### 3. No Income Limits for Contributions
Anyone with earned income can contribute to a Traditional IRA, regardless of how much they make.

## How to Choose: Decision Framework

### Choose Roth IRA if:
✅ You're in a **low tax bracket now** (under 22%)
✅ You're **young** and have decades for tax-free growth
✅ You want **flexibility** — possible need to access funds early
✅ You expect **tax rates to rise** in the future
✅ You have **no workplace retirement plan** and want simplicity
✅ You want to pass wealth to **heirs tax-free**

### Choose Traditional IRA if:
✅ You're in a **high tax bracket now** (32%+)
✅ You expect to be in a **lower tax bracket in retirement**
✅ You want to **reduce taxable income** this year
✅ You're a **high earner** and can't contribute to Roth (consider Backdoor Roth)

### Do Both if:
✅ You're under 50 and your budget allows $7,000 split between accounts
✅ You're uncertain about future tax rates (hedge by diversifying tax treatment)

## Real Numbers: The Roth vs Traditional Comparison

Let's compare a 30-year-old contributing $7,000/year for 35 years, earning 8% average annual return:

**Portfolio at retirement: ~$1,228,000**

| Scenario | Tax Rate Now | Tax Rate in Retirement | Net After-Tax Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roth IRA | 22% (pay now) | n/a | **$1,228,000** |
| Traditional IRA | 22% (save now) | 22% (pay later) | **$957,000** |
| Traditional IRA | 22% (save now) | 15% (pay later) | **$1,044,000** |
| Traditional IRA | 22% (save now) | 30% (pay later) | **$860,000** |

*Key insight:* If your tax rate stays the same, the accounts are equivalent. If taxes go up, Roth wins. If taxes go down, Traditional wins.

## The Backdoor Roth IRA (For High Earners)

If your income exceeds Roth IRA limits, you can still get Roth benefits through a two-step process:
1. Make a **non-deductible contribution** to a Traditional IRA
2. Immediately **convert** that Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA

This is legal, well-documented, and widely used. There are some complexities (the "pro-rata rule") if you have other traditional IRA assets — consult a financial advisor if this applies to you.

## Roth IRA vs Roth 401(k)

Don't confuse these:
- **Roth IRA:** Individual account you open yourself, $7,000 limit, subject to income limits
- **Roth 401(k):** Employer-sponsored, $23,500 limit (2026), **no income limits**

If you have access to a Roth 401(k) through work and are a high earner, this is often the better choice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I have both a 401(k) and an IRA?**
Yes! Contribute to your 401(k) at work AND an IRA. The contribution limits are separate.

**Can I withdraw Roth IRA contributions early?**
Yes — you can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time, tax and penalty-free. Earnings require age 59½ and 5-year rule.

**What if I contribute to the wrong account?**
You can recharacterize contributions between Roth and Traditional IRA up to the tax filing deadline.

## The Bottom Line

For most people in their 20s and 30s earning under $100,000, the **Roth IRA is the clear winner** — you're likely in a lower bracket now than you will be in retirement, and tax-free growth over 30+ years is extraordinarily powerful.

For high earners (32%+ bracket), the **Traditional IRA (or Backdoor Roth)** often makes more sense.

When in doubt, split the contribution. Having both tax-free (Roth) and tax-deferred (Traditional) assets gives you maximum flexibility to manage your taxes in retirement.

The most important thing? Just open an account and start contributing. Time in the market matters far more than which account you choose.
