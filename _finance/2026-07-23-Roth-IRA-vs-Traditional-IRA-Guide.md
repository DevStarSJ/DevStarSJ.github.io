# Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA: Which Retirement Account Should You Choose?

Choosing between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA is one of the most consequential personal finance decisions you'll make. Both are powerful retirement savings vehicles with significant tax advantages — but they work in opposite ways. Getting this right can save you tens of thousands of dollars in taxes over your lifetime.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay Taxes

This is everything:

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributions** | Pre-tax (tax deductible*) | After-tax (no deduction) |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals** | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free (after age 59½) |
| **RMDs** | Required at age 73 | None during owner's lifetime |

\*Deductibility depends on income and whether you have a workplace retirement plan.

**In plain English:**
- **Traditional IRA:** Get a tax break *now*, pay taxes *later* when you withdraw
- **Roth IRA:** Pay taxes *now*, get completely tax-free growth and withdrawals *later*

---

## 2026 Contribution Limits

- **Under 50:** $7,000/year
- **Age 50+:** $8,000/year (catch-up contribution)

These limits apply across *all* your IRAs combined — not per account.

---

## Roth IRA Income Limits (2026)

The Roth IRA has income eligibility restrictions:

| Filing Status | Full Contribution | Partial Contribution | No Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | Below $146,000 | $146,000–$161,000 | Above $161,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | Below $230,000 | $230,000–$240,000 | Above $240,000 |

> **High earner workaround:** The **Backdoor Roth IRA** allows you to contribute to a Traditional IRA (nondeductible) and immediately convert it to a Roth. Consult a tax advisor if you plan to use this strategy.

---

## Traditional IRA Deductibility

If you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan (401k, 403b, etc.), your ability to deduct Traditional IRA contributions may be limited:

| Filing Status | Full Deduction | Partial | No Deduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single (has workplace plan) | Below $79,000 | $79,000–$89,000 | Above $89,000 |
| Married (both have plan) | Below $126,000 | $126,000–$146,000 | Above $146,000 |

If you *can't* deduct a Traditional IRA contribution, a Roth IRA is almost always better.

## Which Is Better? The Key Decision Factors

### Factor 1: Your Tax Rate Now vs. Later

This is the central question.

**Choose Roth IRA if:**
- You're in a **low tax bracket now** and expect to be in a higher bracket later
- You're early in your career (likely your lowest-earning years)
- You expect tax rates to **rise** in the future (a real possibility given national debt levels)
- You want **tax diversification** in retirement

**Choose Traditional IRA if:**
- You're in a **high tax bracket now** and expect lower income in retirement
- You're a high earner who can take a meaningful deduction today
- You need the current-year tax break to afford maximum contributions

### Factor 2: Flexibility and Withdrawal Rules

**Roth IRA is more flexible:**
- Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn **anytime, penalty-free**
- No Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) — let it grow as long as you live
- Can be left to heirs tax-free

**Traditional IRA is more restrictive:**
- Withdrawals before age 59½ incur 10% penalty + taxes
- Required Minimum Distributions start at age 73
- Withdrawals are fully taxable

### Factor 3: Time Horizon

The longer your money stays invested, the more valuable tax-free growth becomes. A 25-year-old has 35+ years of compounding. At 7% growth:

- $7,000 invested today → **~$74,000 in 35 years**
- In a Roth: you keep all $74,000 at withdrawal
- In a Traditional: you pay taxes on all $74,000 at withdrawal (could be 22–32%+ tax bracket)

### Factor 4: Estate Planning

Roth IRAs pass to heirs **income-tax-free**. Traditional IRAs pass a tax burden to your beneficiaries. If leaving a legacy matters to you, Roth is superior.

---

## The Case for Both: IRA Diversification

If you can't decide, consider the **IRA Blending Strategy:**

Split contributions between Traditional and Roth based on your current tax situation. This gives you:
- Tax deductions now (from Traditional)
- Tax-free income in retirement (from Roth)
- Flexibility to pull from either account based on tax efficiency in any given year

Many financial advisors recommend having **both** types of accounts to maximize flexibility in retirement.

## Action Plan Based on Your Situation

| Your Situation | Recommended Choice |
|---|---|
| Early career, low income | **Roth IRA** — maximize tax-free growth |
| Peak earning years, high tax bracket | **Traditional IRA** (deductible) |
| Can't deduct Traditional contributions | **Roth IRA** — always better than non-deductible Traditional |
| Near retirement, uncertain future taxes | **Both** — diversify |
| High earner above Roth limits | **Backdoor Roth** via Traditional IRA |
| Want to leave money to kids/heirs | **Roth IRA** — tax-free inheritance |

## The Bottom Line

For most people under 40 with moderate income: **max out your Roth IRA first.** Tax-free growth for 30+ years is extraordinarily valuable, and the flexibility of Roth contributions makes it a powerful emergency backstop too.

For high earners in peak earning years: **Traditional IRA or Backdoor Roth** depending on your deductibility situation.

When in doubt: do both, split the contribution, and give yourself options in retirement.

The best IRA is the one you actually fund — consistently, every year, starting now.
