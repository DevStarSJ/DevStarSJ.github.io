The Roth vs Traditional IRA debate is one of the most common questions in personal finance. The good news: both are excellent retirement tools. The better news: there's a logical framework to figure out which one works best for *you*.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay Taxes

| Feature                    | Traditional IRA         | Roth IRA               |
|---------------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Contributions              | Pre-tax (tax deductible)| After-tax            |
| Growth                     | Tax-deferred           | Tax-free             |
| Withdrawals in retirement  | Taxed as income        | Tax-free             |
| Required Minimum Distributions | Yes (age 73)     | No                   |
| Early withdrawal penalty   | Yes (before 59½)       | Contributions anytime* |

*You can withdraw Roth *contributions* (not earnings) anytime, penalty-free.

## The Simple Rule

**Choose Traditional if:** You're in a high tax bracket now and expect to be in a lower bracket in retirement.

**Choose Roth if:** You're in a lower bracket now and expect to be in the same or higher bracket in retirement.

It's really about one question: **Will your tax rate be higher now, or higher later?**

## Current 2026 Income Limits

**Roth IRA Phase-out (2026 estimates):**
- Single: $146,000–$161,000
- Married filing jointly: $230,000–$240,000

**Traditional IRA Deductibility (if workplace plan exists):**
- Single: Phase-out at $77,000–$87,000
- Married: Phase-out at $123,000–$143,000

**Annual contribution limit (2026):** $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50+)

## Case-by-Case Analysis

### You're in Your 20s–30s (Typical)
**Likely answer: Roth**

You're probably in the 12–22% tax bracket. You have 30–40 years for tax-free growth. Paying taxes now (at a low rate) and never again is a massive long-term win.

$6,000 invested at age 25, growing at 8% for 40 years = ~$130,000. Tax-free.

### You're in a High-Income Year
**Likely answer: Traditional**

If you're a surgeon making $400K this year, the tax deduction is extremely valuable. Take it now. You can always convert to Roth in a lower-income year (Roth conversion strategy).

### You Expect Social Security + Pension Income
**Likely answer: Traditional with caution**

If you'll have substantial fixed income in retirement, adding RMDs from a Traditional IRA could push you into a higher bracket. Roth can provide tax-free income to avoid this.

### You're Self-Employed
**Consider both**

A SEP-IRA or Solo 401k gives higher contribution limits than a regular IRA. Roth can supplement for tax diversification.

## The Backdoor Roth IRA

If your income is too high for a Roth IRA, you can still use the **Backdoor Roth strategy:**
1. Contribute to a non-deductible Traditional IRA ($7,000)
2. Immediately convert it to Roth
3. Pay tax only on any earnings (usually near zero if done quickly)

This is legal and widely used by high earners. Consult a tax advisor for your specific situation.

## Roth Conversion Ladder

If you have a large Traditional IRA/401k and want to reduce future tax burden, you can convert portions to Roth each year during low-income years (between retirement and age 73 RMDs, for example).

Pay moderate taxes now → no RMDs → tax-free withdrawals forever.

## Real Numbers: Roth vs Traditional Over 30 Years

Assumptions: $7,000/year contribution, 7% return, 22% current bracket, 24% retirement bracket

| Account Type   | Contribution | Growth | Ending Value | Tax Due in Retirement | Net |
|---------------|-------------|--------|-------------|---------------------|-----|
| Roth IRA      | $7,000 after-tax | 30 years | $708,000 | $0 | $708,000 |
| Traditional   | $7,000 pre-tax   | 30 years | $708,000 | ~$170,000 (24%) | ~$538,000 |

In this scenario (higher bracket in retirement), Roth wins by ~$170,000.

Flip the brackets and Traditional wins. This is why the tax bracket question matters.

## What About a 401k?

Your employer's 401k often comes before IRA decisions:
1. **Contribute enough to get full employer match** (free money — always do this)
2. **Max Roth IRA** ($7,000/year) — if eligible
3. **Come back and max 401k** ($23,500 limit in 2026)
4. **Taxable brokerage** — for anything beyond that

## Don't Overthink It

The best retirement account is the one you actually contribute to. If you're paralyzed by the Roth vs Traditional debate, just pick Roth — it's the right call for most people under 40.

The real mistake isn't choosing wrong between Roth and Traditional. The real mistake is contributing nothing while waiting to decide.

## Action Plan

**Under $100K income (single) / $200K (married):**
→ Open Roth IRA immediately. Contribute $583/month to max it.

**Over income limits:**
→ Contribute to 401k first, then backdoor Roth if advantageous.

**Over 50:**
→ Take the $1,000 catch-up contribution ($8,000 total). Every year.

**Have both a 401k and IRA?**
→ Tax diversification: use Traditional 401k + Roth IRA (or vice versa) to hedge against future tax changes.

The goal isn't to be perfect. The goal is to start, and let compound growth do the rest.
