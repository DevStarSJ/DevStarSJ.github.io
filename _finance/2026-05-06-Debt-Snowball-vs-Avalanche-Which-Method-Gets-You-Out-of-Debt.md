Carrying multiple debts is one of the most stressful financial situations you can be in. But beyond paying the minimum on everything and hoping for the best, there are two battle-tested strategies for getting out of debt systematically. They're called the **Snowball** and the **Avalanche** — and the debate between them is one of personal finance's most useful arguments.

## The Setup: What Both Methods Share

Both methods require you to:
1. List all your debts
2. Make minimum payments on all of them
3. Put any "extra" money toward one debt at a time

The difference is in **which debt** gets the extra payment.

## Method 1: The Debt Snowball

**Focus on the smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate.**

Once the smallest debt is paid off, roll that payment into the next-smallest. The "snowball" grows as it rolls — each payoff frees up more money for the next debt.

### Example:

| Debt | Balance | Interest Rate | Minimum |
|------|---------|--------------|---------|
| Medical bill | $800 | 0% | $50 |
| Credit card A | $2,200 | 18% | $65 |
| Car loan | $8,500 | 6% | $180 |
| Student loan | $22,000 | 5% | $240 |

**With $200 extra/month:**
- Month 1–4: Pay $250/month → Medical bill gone. Now roll $250 extra to credit card A.
- Month 5–11: Pay $315/month on credit card A → Paid off. Roll $315 to car loan.
- And so on.

### Why It Works
The psychological reward of eliminating a whole debt — seeing a balance hit $0 — is powerful. Research by Harvard Business School found that people are more motivated when they see progress on a goal, not just when they're being mathematically optimal.

Dave Ramsey famously advocates the Snowball. Behavior, not math, is the hard part of debt payoff.

**Best for:** People who struggle with motivation, have lots of small debts, or need wins to stay committed.

## Method 2: The Debt Avalanche

**Focus on the highest interest rate first, regardless of balance.**

Pay minimums on everything, put extra money toward the highest-rate debt. Once it's gone, move to the next highest rate.

### Using the same example above:

**With $200 extra/month, target by rate:**
1. Credit Card A (18%) first
2. Medical bill (0%) next... wait, this would actually be last since it's 0%
3. Car loan (6%)
4. Student loan (5%)

### Why It Works
Mathematically, you pay less total interest. The highest-rate debt is the most expensive debt — eliminating it first saves you the most money.

**Best for:** Mathematically motivated people, those with large high-interest debts, or anyone who can stay disciplined without quick wins.

## Head-to-Head Comparison

| Factor | Snowball | Avalanche |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| Total interest paid | More | Less |
| Time to debt-free | Slightly longer | Slightly faster |
| Psychological boost | High (quick wins) | Lower (longer wait) |
| Completion rate | Higher | Lower |
| Best for | Motivation-driven | Math-driven |

**The real numbers:** In most cases, the difference in total interest between Snowball and Avalanche is a few hundred to a few thousand dollars over the entire payoff period. Significant, but not life-changing.

The bigger risk is **not sticking to any plan at all.** A Snowball you actually complete beats an Avalanche you abandon.

## A Hybrid Approach

Many financial experts now recommend a hybrid:

1. Start with the Snowball to get your first win quickly
2. Switch to the Avalanche once you're in the habit and motivated

Or: Pay off one or two small debts to get momentum, then pivot to tackling high-interest debt.

## The Numbers That Actually Matter

Before choosing, check your actual interest rates. Often the emotional weight of a debt doesn't match its financial cost:

- **Credit cards (18–29%):** Always a top priority
- **Personal loans (10–20%):** High priority
- **Auto loans (5–8%):** Moderate priority
- **Student loans (4–7%):** Lower priority
- **Mortgage (3–7%):** Often lowest priority; some argue never to prepay

Any debt above 7–8% is costing you more than your investments are likely earning. Pay it off aggressively. Below 5%, the calculus gets complicated.

## What Not to Do

❌ **Paying only minimums** — You'll be in debt for years paying mostly interest  
❌ **Closing cards after paying them off** — Hurts your credit score; keep them open  
❌ **Taking on new debt while paying off old** — You're filling a leaking bucket  
❌ **Ignoring debt to invest instead** — Only makes sense if debt rate is very low  

## Bottom Line

Both the Snowball and Avalanche work. The Avalanche saves more money in theory. The Snowball works better for most people in practice. Choose based on your psychology: do you need quick wins to stay motivated, or are you perfectly fine optimizing for math?

The best strategy is the one you'll actually stick with. Pick one, automate the minimum payments, and throw every extra dollar at your target debt.
