"Should I put money in my Roth IRA or max out my 401(k) first?" It's one of the most common personal finance questions — and the answer is: **it depends, but there's a smart order to follow**.

Both accounts offer powerful tax advantages that can add hundreds of thousands of dollars to your retirement wealth. Understanding how each works — and when to prioritize which — is one of the most important financial decisions you'll make.

## The Key Difference: When You Pay Taxes

This is the fundamental distinction between the two account types:

| | 401(k) Traditional | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributions** | Pre-tax (reduces taxable income now) | After-tax (no tax break now) |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals in retirement** | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free |
| **Required Minimum Distributions** | Yes, starting at age 73 | No |
| **Income limits** | None | Phase out at $146K–$161K (single) |

**401(k)**: You get a tax break today. Pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement.  
**Roth IRA**: No tax break today. Never pay taxes on that money again.

## 2026 Contribution Limits

- **401(k)**: $23,500/year ($31,000 if age 50+)
- **IRA (Roth or Traditional)**: $7,000/year ($8,000 if age 50+)
- **Roth IRA income phase-out**: Single $146K–$161K | Married $230K–$240K

## The Optimal Order: A Framework

Here's the proven order for maximizing retirement accounts:

### Step 1: 401(k) up to employer match
If your employer matches contributions — **always capture the full match first**. A 50% match on up to 6% of salary is a guaranteed 50% return. Nothing beats free money.

Example: Salary $80,000, employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6%  
→ Contribute 6% = $4,800 → Employer adds $2,400 → Instant 50% return

### Step 2: Max your Roth IRA ($7,000)
If you've captured the full match, now prioritize the Roth IRA. Why?
- **Tax-free growth and withdrawals** forever
- **No RMDs** — money grows as long as you live, you decide when to withdraw
- **Flexible**: You can withdraw Roth *contributions* (not gains) at any time without penalty — a secondary emergency fund
- **Best accounts**: Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab (all charge $0 fees, excellent index funds)

### Step 3: Go back and max the 401(k) ($23,500)
After maxing the Roth IRA, return to the 401(k) and increase contributions up to the annual limit.

### Step 4: Taxable brokerage account
Once you've maxed all tax-advantaged accounts, invest additional savings in a taxable brokerage.

## The Roth Advantage Is Largest When…

**Prioritize Roth when:**
- You're in a low tax bracket now (early career, low income year)
- You expect higher taxes in retirement
- You're young — decades of tax-free compounding ahead
- You've already used tax-deferred accounts heavily and want tax diversification
- Tax rates are likely to rise in the future (historically, they often do)

**Prioritize Traditional 401(k) when:**
- You're in a high tax bracket (35%–37%) and need the deduction now
- You expect lower income (and lower taxes) in retirement
- You're close to retirement (less time for Roth tax-free growth to accumulate)

## The Power of a Roth: Tax-Free Compounding

Invest $7,000/year in a Roth IRA earning 9% annually, starting at age 25:

- Age 65: **$2,980,000** — completely tax-free
- If in a traditional IRA at 22% tax rate: only ~$2,324,000 after taxes

The difference: **$656,000** — that's the value of tax-free growth over 40 years.

## What If You're Self-Employed?

Self-employed individuals can use:
- **SEP-IRA**: Up to 25% of net self-employment income, max $69,000 in 2026
- **Solo 401(k)**: Same limits as SEP-IRA but allows Roth contributions
- **SIMPLE IRA**: Lower limits, for businesses with employees

The Solo 401(k) with Roth option is often best for high-earning self-employed individuals.

## Quick Checklist: Am I On Track?

General retirement savings benchmarks:
- Age 30: 1× annual salary saved
- Age 40: 3× annual salary saved
- Age 50: 6× annual salary saved
- Age 60: 8× annual salary saved
- Age 67: 10× annual salary saved

If you're behind: **increase contributions immediately** and consider catching up with the age 50+ limits.

## Action Plan for 2026

1. **Confirm your employer's 401(k) match** — and make sure you're contributing enough to capture all of it
2. **Open a Roth IRA** if you don't have one (Fidelity or Vanguard — 10 minutes, $0 minimum)
3. **Set up automatic monthly contributions** to your Roth IRA ($583/month = $7,000/year)
4. **Choose index funds**: Total Stock Market or S&P 500 fund in both accounts
5. **Increase 401(k) contributions** toward the $23,500 limit as income grows

Both accounts are powerful. Used together, with the right sequencing, they create a two-pronged tax advantage — lower taxes today AND tax-free income in retirement. Start funding both as early as possible. Your 65-year-old self will thank you.
