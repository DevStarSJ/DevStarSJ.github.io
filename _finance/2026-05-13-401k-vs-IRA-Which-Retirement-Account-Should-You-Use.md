# 401(k) vs IRA: Which Retirement Account Should You Use?

If you're building for retirement in the United States, you'll likely encounter two types of accounts more than any others: the **401(k)** and the **IRA**. Both offer significant tax advantages. Both are essential tools. And despite some overlap, they work differently enough that knowing when to use each one matters.

The good news: you don't have to choose. Most people should use both.

## The Quick Summary

| Feature | 401(k) | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who offers it | Employer | You open it | You open it |
| 2025 contribution limit | $23,500 | $7,000 | $7,000 |
| Catch-up (50+) | +$7,500 | +$1,000 | +$1,000 |
| Tax on contributions | Pre-tax | Pre-tax (if eligible) | After-tax |
| Tax on withdrawals | Taxed as income | Taxed as income | Tax-free |
| Employer match | Yes (often) | No | No |
| Income limits | None | Phase-out limits | Phase-out limits |
| Investment choices | Limited to plan options | Broad (choose your own) | Broad (choose your own) |
| Required minimum dist. | Yes (age 73) | Yes (age 73) | No |

## Understanding the 401(k)

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan. Money comes out of your paycheck **before** taxes are taken, reducing your taxable income today.

### The Employer Match: Free Money

If your employer offers a match, contribute at least enough to get all of it — before anything else. A typical match is 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary.

**Example:**
- Salary: $60,000
- Contribute 6% = $3,600/year
- Employer matches 50% = $1,800/year free
- **Total invested: $5,400 for your $3,600 contribution**

Not capturing the full match is the closest thing to leaving free money on the table that exists in personal finance.

### 401(k) Limitations

- **Limited investment choices**: You're restricted to whatever funds the plan offers (typically 10–30 options)
- **Higher fees possible**: Some 401(k) plans carry high expense ratios — check your fund costs
- **Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)**: Starting at age 73, you must withdraw a minimum each year regardless of need

## Understanding the IRA

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is opened by you, not through an employer. There are two main types:

### Traditional IRA
- Contributions may be **tax-deductible** (depends on income and whether you have a 401k at work)
- Grows tax-deferred
- Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income
- Same RMD rules as 401(k)

### Roth IRA
- Contributions are **after-tax** (no deduction now)
- Grows **tax-free**
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are **completely tax-free**
- **No RMDs** — your money can grow indefinitely
- Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time, penalty-free

### IRA Advantages Over 401(k)
- **Unlimited investment choice**: Choose any stock, ETF, fund available at your brokerage
- **Lower fees**: Shop for the best low-cost provider
- **Roth option**: Tax-free growth is extremely valuable, especially for younger investors

### IRA Income Limits (2025)
Roth IRA contributions phase out at:
- Single: $150,000–$165,000 MAGI
- Married filing jointly: $236,000–$246,000 MAGI

Traditional IRA deductibility phases out if you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan:
- Single: $79,000–$89,000
- Married: $126,000–$146,000

## The Optimal Strategy: Priority Order

### Step 1: 401(k) up to employer match
Always, always, always. Capture every dollar of free employer match first.

### Step 2: Max out your Roth IRA
Once you've captured the full match, fill your Roth IRA to the annual limit ($7,000). The tax-free growth over decades is unbeatable.

### Step 3: Return to 401(k) for additional contributions
If you still have money to invest after the Roth IRA, contribute more to your 401(k) up to the annual limit ($23,500).

### Step 4: Taxable brokerage account
After maxing tax-advantaged accounts, open a regular brokerage account for additional investing.

## Traditional vs Roth: Which Tax Treatment Wins?

The fundamental question: **Do you pay taxes now (Roth) or later (Traditional)?**

| Situation | Better Choice |
|---|---|
| Young / early career / lower tax bracket now | Roth |
| Peak earning years / high tax bracket now | Traditional |
| Expect higher income in retirement | Roth |
| Expect lower income in retirement | Traditional |
| Uncertain about future taxes | Split between both |
| High income (above Roth limits) | Traditional 401(k) + consider Backdoor Roth |

**General rule for most people**: Roth is better when you're young (lower current income, more time for tax-free growth). Traditional becomes more attractive in high-earning years.

## Common Mistakes

**Not contributing enough to get the full match**: If your company matches 50% up to 6%, and you contribute 4%, you're losing 1% of your salary in free money.

**Cashing out when changing jobs**: Rolling over a 401(k) to an IRA when you change jobs is almost always better than cashing out (you'd owe taxes + a 10% early withdrawal penalty).

**Ignoring fees**: A 1% higher expense ratio in your 401(k) can cost you $100,000+ over a career. Check what you're paying.

**Not increasing contributions with raises**: Every raise is an opportunity to increase your retirement contribution rate. Make it automatic.

**Forgetting about catch-up contributions**: If you're 50 or older, you can contribute significantly more. Take advantage.

## The Bottom Line

The 401(k) and IRA aren't competitors — they're teammates. The employer match makes the 401(k) an obvious first priority. The Roth IRA's tax-free growth makes it the second. Together, they provide the tax-advantaged foundation that most successful retirement plans are built on.

The best time to start was yesterday. The second best time is right now. Contribute something today — even $50 a month — and increase it whenever you can.

Your future self will thank you — in tax-free dollars.
